Threshold 1: The Golden Stairs — ARRIVAL

THE GOLDEN STAIRS

A HOME IS MADE, NOT FOUND.

c. 1895

A boy aboard an immigrant ship in Boston Harbor raises his hand to salute the American flag.

Around him, the adults’ faces are drawn from a transatlantic journey in steerage. A woman holds a small child. A man stands in the dress of the world he has left behind.

But the children are smiling.

In the boy’s eager salute is the hope that carried so many across the Atlantic: that here, their children might have opportunities they had never known.

By the early 1900s, Boston was the nation’s second busiest immigration port after New York. More than half a million newcomers arrived between 1900 and 1910 alone.

1923

Nearly three decades later, passengers crowd the deck of the Adriatic. Trunks, suitcases, and bundles sit between them. They have reached Boston Harbor and are waiting for permission to land.

America had changed.

The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 had imposed limits by nationality. Ships raced to arrive before monthly quotas filled. Those arriving too late could be sent back.

Imagine the waiting. The money spent. The goodbyes already said. For some, rejection could mean returning to the very hardship they had struggled to escape.

An ocean behind them. A new life almost within reach. And still, no certainty where home would be.

Today

You stand at the Golden Stairs, above the waterfront where immigrants arrived at piers including Cunard Wharf. Those requiring further examination or detention could be taken to the East Boston Immigration Station, opened in the shipyard below you in 1920.

The stairs became a symbol of the climb toward “golden opportunity.” For those who settled here, that promise became everyday work: finding a job, raising children, opening a shop, helping a neighbor.

Through those lives, East Boston took shape.

The station is gone, but East Boston remains a gateway to America. More than four million international passengers arrive through Logan each year. Some come to visit. Some return home. Others come hoping to build a life here.

Today, more than four in ten East Boston residents were born outside the United States.

More than a century later, the hope in these photographs still brings people here.

The Golden Thread follows what we make of that hope: the lives we build, the neighbors we become, the home we make together.

The future is ours to make.
